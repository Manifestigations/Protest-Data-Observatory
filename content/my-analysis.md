## A Brief Account of the Past Months

The turnout, arrests and injuries chart shows the early dynamics of the movement quite starkly: school blockades were rapidly spreading around the country (and its overseas territories), which was met with a hugely excessive response from the police at the end of September. This helps to contextualise why people were so angry and why, when the police lost control on Oct 17, they caused so much damage (although accurate figures are very hard to come by).

In an attempt to regain control, the state responded with lawfare, with Macron and his prefects attempting to ban protests and implement curfews for young people. This has been resoundingly rejected week after week as---during the school holidays and therefore a break in the school blockades---huge numbers of people continued to take to the streets in defiance of these orders.

Looking at the data since Oct 17, things seem to have settled into an uneasy routine: the unions declare planned routes; the prefects ban them or propose ridiculous alternatives; the unions file urgent appeals that invariably get rejected; the weaker unions accept the alternative routes; the spicier unions join the Gilets Jaunes, students, et al. in defiant undeclared manifestations; the cops wait until most people go; and then they engage in running battles with the remaining few until the evening. But either the more-recent arrest and injury figures are currently undercounted, or there has been a steady decline in each since Oct 17, suggesting a certain amount of de-escalation. However, in my own experience, that is deceptive: every week, the police here seem to be putting up more barriers of more robust construction, as well as bringing out more and more armoured vehicles and flying their helicopters lower and lower.

---

### Policing

However, I think the police may have decided to try intelligence in place of violence for a change. They seem to be using kettling much more often, surrounding and tear-gassing protestors and then allowing them to exit only by allowing their identity card to be photographed and a fine for attending an illegal protest to be issued. The fixed-penalty fines are 135EUR, but I suspect their main objective is the IDs for intelligence collection and the issuing of future charges. Similarly, police in Brest [seized the photo directories of several schools](https://www.lemonde.fr/societe/article/2026/11/09/a-brest-la-police-saisit-les-trombinoscopes-de-plusieurs-lycees-eleves-et-parents-denoncent-un-fichage_6808694_3224.html), for as-yet-unclear reasons.

It's worth noting that in France, it is illegal for the state to record ethnicity data and for employers to record lists of striking employees, both because of the experience in WWII where the French collaborator and German occupier governments used these lists to round up their enemies for deportation and worse. The fear in the back of everyone's mind is the possibility that France will again have a far-right government next April, which [almost two thirds of the police openly support](https://www.humanite.fr/politique/rassemblement-national/entre-les-67-de-vote-rn-dans-ses-rangs-et-lambiance-securitaire-en-vigueur-la-police-deja-prete-a-servir-lextreme-droite), and so where might those lists end up?

But at the same time, I don't want to give them too much credit. [Falling standards in police training](https://shs.cairn.info/revue-alternatives-non-violentes-2021-1-page-7?lang=fr) and the [inappropriateness of their public order policing methods](https://shs.cairn.info/revue-alternatives-non-violentes-2021-1-page-7?lang=fr) have has been a topic of much discussion in France [and elsewhere](https://www.bisa.ac.uk/members/working-groups/cst/articles/maintaining-order-state-terror-against-people-france) in recent years, but I've seen errors ranging from:

- petty, like upside-down velcro patches
- to quite serious, like [causing a multi-car pileup on the motorway](https://contre-attaque.net/2026/11/14/carambolage-de-crs-ce-samedi-matin-4-fourgons-et-13-policiers-hors-service/) en route to repress a protest
- to very serious, like individual or small groups of riot cops repeatedly getting separated and surrounded by protestors, and even once being [snuck up on and placed (briefly) in a chokehold](https://contre-attaque.net/2026/11/01/acte-iii-du-31-octobre-le-recap-en-images-dune-journee-de-folie/)

If they were facing a real, organised, violent threat, I wouldn't much fancy their chances.

## 2026's Historic Positioning

In terms of the historic comparisons (although the data here is less trustworthy; see the section below), the pension reform protests are interesting as, apparently, the only case where the protests were already ongoing for some time before the police repression began. The most obvious comparison to make is with the Gilets Jaunes (especially since the present movement has adopted the same Act-based structure), which managed to prolong a steadily decreasing weekly turnout for well over a year (and at one point had Macron [considering escape by helicopter](https://www.lavoixdunord.fr/506027/article/2018-12-13/un-helicoptere-etait-pret-samedi-au-cas-ou-pour-emmener-emmanuel-macron)), but it's hard to say what was achieved by doing so.

## What Next?

The big question facing the movement is [what should they do now](https://lepoing.net/manifestation-du-7-novembre-a-montpellier-la-contestation-cherche-encore-sa-strategie/)?

They've seen off wanton police violence, shameless media demonisation and one particularly chaotic night that could have lost them public support (but this seems not to have happened). As I see it (and I'm sure many others are having the same conversations), they need to figure out what they want and develop their theory of change. For example, the government has already made some small concessions to the students' original demands for increased education funding, but the movement has now absorbed all sorts of other grievances, from the cost of living to fear of militarism to personal distaste for Macron's patronising and authoritarian style of rule to fear of an incoming RN government.

Do they want to influence elites? Do they want to focus on gaining institutional power in next year's elections? There is the presidential election in April, which will be followed by a legislative one. In this case, should people unify behind a single presidential candidate (whatever their skepticisms of electoral politics) whilst working to produce something like the Student List in Serbia?

Do they want to inflict economic pain on the state to force it to meet their demands? The data below suggests that we may be reaching an inflection point whereby the cost of policing these protests exceeded even the highest estimated for the damage caused on Oct 17. But as the Gilets Jaunes well know, this is an approach that favours many dispersed, smaller groups acting against intelligently-chosen targets (e.g. malls, logistics hubs, etc.), and not just smashing up random shop windows.

What _are_ those demands? Just the original student demands? Or the whole programme of reversing the pension reforms, re-funding every sector, demilitarising the police, seeking justice for Palestine, etc.?

Or do they want to generate mass support, to bring people out in their millions in a general strike? This means addressing the reasons why French people today are less likely to respond to a strike call than previous generations. This means putting money aside for strike funds to help those who would struggle the most financially by giving up their income. This means identifying groups who, once all of the public transport workers are on strike, can take over the collective running of these vital services, [as in Barcelona a century ago](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/gaston-leval-collectives-in-the-spanish-revolution#toc34).

Do they want to overthrow the government by force? This has happened before---the end of Fourth Republic came when French military personnel and settlers in Algeria launched an attempted coup d'état. Certainly, the government seems to be [taking the possibility seriously](https://neru.tv/sources/macron-bunker-secret-pour-fuir-le-peuple/). But even if protestors did succeed in causing Macron to flee, it's not clear how the symbolic victory might translate into a practical takeover.

A common phrase on protestors lips is 'be water', taking inspiration from Bruce Lee and the Hong Kong protest movement. But it echoes [Mao's famous dictum](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/works/1937/guerrilla-warfare/ch06.htm#:~:text=Many%20people%20think%20it%20impossible,its%20native%20element%20cannot%20live%2E), that 'the guerrilla must move amongst the people as a fish swims in the sea.' There is a time for mass, and for overpowering barricades through sheer numbers---the French celebrate one such example as their National Day. But there is also a time for commando tactics, and getting those lines through inconspicuity and subterfuge to launch attacks behind the enemy's rear.

Personally, I thing both approaches are necessary, but neither alone is sufficient. And, for now, I do not see the latter well-represented in the present movement. When they start erecting barriers the night before a protest, where are the rapid response networks who can organise enough people at short notice to physically block the work, buying the lawyers time to block it through legal means?

These debates, and many more, are currently being played out amongst the dizzying constellation of General Assemblies that have sprung up over the past month. There is [a clear drive to build dual power](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=l6FYgPChuN8). But how far will it go?

## Predicting the Future

At the moment, I think a complete fizzling-out is unlikely. I think the most likely course is that this will continue for some time, with slowly diminishing turnout and some small concessions won here and there. Most people's engagement will be limited to therapeutic marching, but the people who are politicised here will go on to develop new associations and be instrumental in future movements, as the former Gilets Jaunes have been here, and the 68-ers before them. Things will remain at a low simmer during the colder, wetter months, before re-emerging from its winter hibernation in time for next year's elections.

But maybe, just maybe, the threat of an incoming fascist government and the memory of so many past underwhelming results will serve to focus minds and harden hearts in time to press home their present advantage and win lasting, significant wins. And just as mere resistance in France has already spread like wildfire across Europe, a definitive victory in France could be the thing to spark a conflagration that burns down the old world and inaugurates the new. We might finally be witnessing an end to [the past fifty years of counter-revolution](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9781635900163/how-the-world-swung-to-the-right/), if this movement (and others) can truly pick up the flame of '68; the Rojava Revolution; the early promise of the Arab Spring; and too many other examples to list here.
